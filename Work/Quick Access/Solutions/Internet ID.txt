## ✅ **1. Your Public IP Address (assigned by your ISP)**

This is the *closest thing* to an “internet ID.”
When you browse the internet, websites see the **public IP address** from your router.

* It is assigned by your **Internet Service Provider (ISP)**
* It may be **dynamic** (changing) or **static** (fixed)
* It identifies your *connection*, not your individual computer

Your PC doesn’t decide this; your ISP does.

---

## ✅ **2. Local Identifiers Inside Your Network**

These exist on your home or office network, not the internet:

### **• Local IP address (e.g., 192.168.1.24)**

Given to your computer by your router via DHCP.

### **• MAC address**

A hardware identifier unique to your network adapter.

* Only visible *inside* your LAN
* Routers do **not** forward your MAC address to the internet
* Websites **never** see your MAC address

---

## ✅ **3. Browser / Software Identifiers**

Apps and browsers build identifiers like:

* Cookies
* Tracking tokens
* Client fingerprints (screen size, fonts, OS, etc.)

These aren’t “Windows-specific,” but they can uniquely identify a device over time.

---

# ✅ 4. Hardware-Level Identifiers (Not Normally Sent to Websites)

Your PC has unique hardware serials, but Windows and browsers **do not expose them to the internet**:

### • **Motherboard serial**

### • **CPU ID** (modern CPUs often mask this)

### • **TPM chip unique ID**

### • **Storage device serial numbers**

These are generally accessible only to:

* Windows internally
* Enterprise management systems (Intune, domain networks)
* Specific drivers
* Security software

They are **not shared through normal internet traffic**.

---

# ✅ 5. Windows Telemetry Identifiers

Windows 11 uses several internal IDs for diagnostic purposes:

### • **Hardware Hash (HWID)**

Used for activation and device registration.

### • **Device GUID**

Every Windows installation has a device-wide GUID for telemetry.

### • **App Installation IDs**

Each app may generate its own ID for crash reports and updates.

None of these are shared with random websites — only Microsoft services and some applications use them.

---

# ✅ 6. Network Stack Identifiers (Low-Level)

These exist at a technical level:

### • **DHCP Client Identifier**

Sent to your router, not to the internet.

### • **Hostname**

Sometimes sent to local networks (e.g., “DESKTOP-123ABC”), but websites don’t normally see it.

### • **TLS Fingerprints**

Your PC’s TLS stack (the thing that handles HTTPS) creates a unique-ish fingerprint based on:

* Supported ciphers
* TLS version
* Extensions
* Order of parameters

Websites *can* see this, and it often uniquely identifies your device type.

---

# ✅ 7. Application-Level IDs

Many apps create their own identifiers and transmit them to their own servers:

* Steam hardware survey ID
* Discord device session ID
* Google Chrome sync ID
* Spotify device ID

These are service-specific and never sent broadly across the internet.

---

# ✅ 8. ISP / Carrier Metadata (Rare but Possible)

Sometimes your ISP may insert identifiers:

### • Carrier-grade NAT tagging

### • ISP connection session tokens

### • Mobile carrier “supercookies” (for phones, not Windows)

Most broadband and fiber providers *do not* use these, but technically it's possible.
