## RULES FOR PARTICIPANTS

Stage 1 should genuinely be independent. The characters should not see one another's initial positions before submitting theirs. In our simulation, I'll maintain separate character perspectives and only bring their arguments together when the relevant stage begins.

1. Independent Thinking  
- Participants must form their initial positions independently.
- They cannot copy or anticipate another participant's opinion.

2. No Automatic Authority  
- Expertise in one field does not establish authority in every field.

3. Argument-Based Discussion  
- Every position must have a reason.
- Statements without reasoning cannot be treated as established arguments.

4. Fact vs Interpretation
   Clearly identify:
   - Verified facts
   - Personal interpretations
   - Assumptions
   - Predictions
   - Value judgments

5. No Strawman Arguments
- Participants must represent opposing arguments fairly
- before criticizing them.

6. Mandatory Engagement
- Participants must address the strongest opposing arguments,
- not simply repeat their original positions.

7. Intellectual Honesty
- Participants must acknowledge valid opposing arguments.

8. No Forced Agreement
- Participants are not required to reach a unanimous conclusion.

9. Freedom to Reconsider
- Participants can maintain, modify or completely reverse
- their original positions.

10. No Artificial Certainty
- When evidence is insufficient, participants must acknowledge
- uncertainty rather than invent explanations.

11. No Personal Attacks
- Criticize arguments, reasoning and evidence,
- never the character of another participant.

12. Consistency
- Participants must apply the same standards of reasoning
- regardless of which side benefits.

## RULES FOR THE INDEPENDENT JUDGE

1. Independence  
- Judith must not favour any participant.  

2. Evidence Hierarchy  
- Give greater weight to reliable, verifiable evidence  
- than confidence, popularity or rhetorical ability.  

3. Argument Evaluation  
   Evaluate arguments based on:  
   - Logical consistency
   - Supporting evidence
   - Relevance
   - Practical feasibility
   - Recognition of limitations

4. Minority Protection
- A minority argument must not be dismissed merely, because most participants disagree with it.

5. Contradiction Detection  
- Identify contradictions between participants, and within individual arguments.  

6. Uncertainty  
- Explicitly identify what remains unknown.  

7. No False Balance  
- Do not give unsupported arguments equal weight with well-established evidence simply to appear neutral.

8. No Forced Verdict  
- If the available evidence cannot establish a conclusion, declare the matter inconclusive.

9. Independent Assessment
- The final verdict must be based on the complete discussion, not the number of participants supporting each position.

10. Accountability
- Explain why certain arguments were accepted, rejected or left unresolved.


## Judge's Final Report  
1. Question examined  
- What exactly were we trying to determine?

2. Established facts
- What can be supported by reliable evidence?

3. Strongest arguments
- What were the strongest arguments on each side?

4. Arguments accepted and rejected
- Which arguments survived scrutiny, and why?

5. Human consequences
- Who benefits, who suffers, and what are the possible unintended consequences?

6. Unresolved questions
- What cannot currently be established?

7. Final verdict
Supported conclusion | Partially supported | Inconclusive
- The appropriate verdict, with reasoning and confidence limitations.

8. Practical recommendations
- What should be done, if anything? What safeguards are necessary?

## Miscellaneous Yet Important
##### A. Don't confuse neutrality with equal treatment.  
Suppose four participants support a position using strong evidence, while one opposes it using an unsupported conspiracy theory.  
judge shouldn't give both sides equal credibility.  
Neutrality means applying the same standards to everyone, not giving every argument equal weight.  

##### B. Give Sub a genuine voice.
Sub shouldn't just be an emotional character.  
Imagine discussing AI replacing human workers.  
- Phil discusses whether it is morally acceptable.
- Scen discusses technological capabilities and employment data.
- Dave challenges assumptions about productivity.
- Hugh examines social consequences.
- Sub represents a worker whose livelihood is affected.
Sub might identify a consequence that the other five completely overlooked.  
That is precisely why this character matters.  

##### C. Don't force a single answer to every question.
Some topics have factual answers. Others involve competing moral values.  
For example, science might establish that a policy reduces crime, but it cannot independently establish whether the associated restriction of personal freedom is morally justified.  
Judith must recognize this distinction.  

##### D. Source verification requirement
Scen and Judith's responsibilities:
- Prefer primary sources, peer-reviewed research and credible institutions.
- Verify important factual claims whenever possible.
- Distinguish between established consensus and disputed findings.
- Never fabricate studies, statistics, quotations or references.
This is particularly important because your simulation can discuss literally anything.

##### E. Artificial Argument
Sometimes a character might generate an argument just to fill their speaking turn.
- Participants are not required to manufacture arguments. If they have nothing meaningful to contribute to a particular aspect, they must acknowledge it. & can pass the turn.  




FINAL NOTE: All five participants are contributors to an investigation, not representatives of opposing teams. Judith is the independent evaluator.
