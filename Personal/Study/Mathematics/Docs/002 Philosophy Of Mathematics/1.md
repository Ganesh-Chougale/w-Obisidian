## Philosophy of mathematics
- Math began as a tool: Humans developed mathematics to count, trade, measure, and describe the world.
- Numbers are abstract concepts: The number 5 represents a quantity, like five apples.
- Symbols are human-made: We use symbols like \\(+\\), \\(-\\), and \\(=\\) to express mathematical ideas.
- Number systems are conventional: Base 10 is common because humans have ten fingers, but other bases work too.

### 1. Addition vs. subtraction

Addition combines quantities.

\\[ 2+3=5 \\]

Two apples plus three apples gives five apples.

Subtraction removes or compares quantities.

\\[ 5-2=3 \\]

Five apples minus two apples leaves three.

### 2. Why does order matter?

Addition: order doesn't matter.

\\[ 2+3=3+2=5 \\]

Imagine two apples and three apples. Switching the groups doesn't change the total. You still have five apples.

This is called the commutative property.

Subtraction: order does matter.

\\[ 5-2=3 \\]

But:

\\[ 2-5=-3 \\]

Imagine having five apples and giving away two. You have three left. But if you have only two apples and give away five, you are short by three. The result changes because the starting quantity changes.

Subtraction is not commutative.

### 3. Why does grouping matter?

Addition: grouping doesn't matter.

\\[ (2+3)+4=2+(3+4)=9 \\]

You can combine two apples with three first, or three with four first. The total remains nine.

This is called the associative property.

Subtraction: grouping does matter.

\\[ (10-5)-2=3 \\]

But:

\\[ 10-(5-2)=7 \\]

Why? In the first expression, you subtract five from ten, then subtract two. In the second, you calculate the difference between five and two first, then subtract that result from ten.

The parentheses change what you calculate first.

### 4. Quick comparison

| Property              | Addition              | Subtraction               |
| --------------------- | --------------------- | ------------------------- |
| Order matters?        | No                    | Yes                       |
| Grouping matters?     | No                    | Yes                       |
| Example               | \\(2+3=3+2\\)         | \\(5-2\ne2-5\\)           |
| Example with grouping | \\((2+3)+4=2+(3+4)\\) | \\((10-5)-2\ne10-(5-2)\\) |

### 5. The philosophical idea

Mathematical symbols aren't arbitrary decorations. They represent operations and relationships.

- Addition combines quantities, so switching or regrouping them preserves the total.
- Subtraction measures what remains or the difference between quantities, so changing the order or grouping can change the result.

Core takeaway: 
- `Understanding why a mathematical rule works is more powerful than memorizing the rule.`
- `Maths suppose to be operations as intelligible and relatable.`
