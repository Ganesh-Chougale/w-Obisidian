# Examples of Lateral Thinking
## 1. Truck stucked under the bridge
```txt
A large delivery truck gets stuck under a low stone bridge. The truck is too tall to pass through, and traffic starts building up for miles. Police, emergency workers, and engineers arrive to find a way to get the truck out.

Normal Thinking: focuses on the problem directly.
The engineers look at the exact place where the truck is stuck. They check if they can break some of the bridge. They also think about using big machines to lift the bridge a little or cutting the top of the truck. These ideas might work, but they would take a lot of time, money, and effort—and could damage the bridge or the truck.

Lateral Thinking: looks at the problem in a different way.
A young boy walking past the traffic jam looks at the truck and asks one of the engineers:
"Why don't you let some air out of the tires?"
The engineers try it. As the air comes out, the truck becomes a little lower. It drops just enough to fit under the bridge. The driver can then slowly reverse the truck out.

Normal thinking focused on the top of the truck and tried to change the bridge or the truck.
Lateral thinking looked for something else that could be changed. Instead of fighting the main problem, it found a simple solution somewhere else.

That's the power of lateral thinking: when the obvious solution is difficult, look at the problem from a different angle.
```
## 2. Slow Elevator
```txt
A hotel had a simple problem.
Guests kept complaining that the elevator was too slow.

The hotel owner thought the solution was obvious: "We need to make the elevator faster."

He looked into buying a faster elevator, but that would be very expensive.

Then someone suggested a completely different idea.
Instead of making the elevator faster, they put mirrors inside the elevator.

Now something interesting happened.
Guests spent the short elevator ride looking at themselves in the mirror. They checked their hair, clothes, or makeup.
People stopped complaining about the elevator.
The elevator was still just as slow as before.
Nothing about the elevator had actually changed.
What changed was how people felt about the waiting time.

Normal thinking asked:
"How can we make the elevator faster?"

Lateral thinking asked:
"How can we make people stop noticing that the elevator is slow?"

The clever solution was not to fix the elevator.
It was to change the problem.
```   

## 3. The 9 AM Server Crash
```txt
A company's internal software system kept crashing every morning exactly at 9:00 AM.
Thousands of employees were arriving at work and logging into the application at the exact same moment, instantly overloading the database.

Normal Thinking: focused on strengthening the system.
The backend engineers and database administrators looked at upgrading the hardware. They considered buying larger servers, adding more memory, and rewriting the SQL queries to execute faster. This would cost the company thousands of dollars and take weeks to implement.

Lateral Thinking: looked at the behavior causing the load.
Instead of changing the servers, the company changed the login process. They programmed the client application to add a random delay of 1 to 60 seconds before sending the login request to the server. 

The crashes stopped immediately. The existing database was perfectly capable of handling the daily volume of users, just not all in the exact same millisecond.

Normal thinking tried to build a massive pipe to handle a sudden flood.
Lateral thinking smoothed out the flood so the existing pipe worked perfectly.

The clever solution wasn't to write faster code or buy bigger servers.
It was to change how the traffic arrived.
```
